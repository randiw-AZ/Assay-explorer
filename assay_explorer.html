Class example: Thyroid hormone

The idea behind RIA: a fixed amount of antibody (Ab) is offered a fixed amount of radiolabeled hormone (*H) — enough to fill every binding site. When unlabeled hormone (H) is introduced, it competes with *H for those same sites. The more unlabeled hormone present, the less *H ends up bound. Measuring how much *H is bound (versus free) tells you, indirectly, how much unlabeled hormone was there to begin with.

Because every step changes what gets measured downstream, getting the sequence right — and understanding what each step protects against — matters more than memorizing it.

Ab — primary antibody, fixed amount
*H — radiolabeled hormone (tracer)
H — unlabeled hormone (standard or patient sample)
Bound fraction — Ab-hormone complex, becomes the pellet
Free fraction — unbound hormone, stays in the supernatant
Standard curve — bound signal plotted against known H concentrations

    Class example: Estradiol

    The idea behind competitive ELISA: instead of a radioactive tracer, the "label" is an enzyme (horseradish peroxidase, HRP) attached to hormone. A fixed amount of antibody is coated onto the plate, and unlabeled hormone (from a standard or a patient sample) competes with HRP-labeled hormone for those antibody sites. After washing away everything that isn't bound, a substrate is added that the enzyme converts into a colored product — so the amount of color reflects how much labeled hormone stayed bound.

    Just like RIA, this is a competitive assay: the more unlabeled hormone present, the less labeled hormone ends up bound, and the weaker the color signal. Less color means more hormone in the sample.

    Ab — antibody coated onto the well
    H — unlabeled hormone (standard or patient sample)
    HRP–H — enzyme-conjugated (labeled) hormone
    TMB — substrate that turns color when acted on by HRP
    Stop solution (acid) — halts the color reaction at a fixed point
    OD450 — optical density read at 450 nm; proportional to bound HRP–H

      Class example: Insulin

      The idea behind sandwich ELISA: a fixed primary antibody (Ab1) coats the well and captures hormone from the sample. A second antibody (Ab2), labeled with biotin, then binds a different site on that same captured hormone molecule — sandwiching the hormone between two antibodies. An enzyme (HRP) is recruited onto the biotin, and it's that enzyme that eventually produces the color signal.

      This is a key contrast with RIA and competitive ELISA: there's no competition here. More hormone in the sample means more sandwich complexes form, which means more enzyme gets recruited, which means more color — OD is directly proportional to hormone concentration, not inversely proportional. (It's also why sandwich ELISA only works for hormones large enough to have two separate antibody-binding sites — small hormones like steroids typically need a competitive format instead.)

      Ab1 — primary (capture) antibody coated on the well
      H — hormone (standard or patient sample); needs two distinct binding sites
      Ab2–biotin — secondary (detection) antibody, labeled with biotin
      HRP — enzyme that binds the biotin label and generates the color signal
      TMB — substrate that turns color when acted on by HRP
      OD450 — optical density read at 450 nm; directly proportional to hormone present

        Class example: hCG — home pregnancy test

        The idea behind a lateral flow assay: unlike the first three assays, there's no technician mixing reagents step by step. Everything is pre-loaded onto the strip, and capillary action does the work once a sample is applied. Urine is applied at the reaction site, where a free (mobile) anti-hCG antibody linked to a dye enzyme is waiting. If hCG is present, it binds this antibody as both are carried along the strip.

        At the test site, a fixed anti-hCG antibody captures any hCG–antibody complexes that reach it, and the enzyme they carry converts TMB from clear to blue — producing the visible test line. Any free anti-hCG antibody left over continues on to the control site, where a fixed anti-primary antibody captures the free anti-hCG antibody even though it's not carrying hCG, turning that TMB blue as well. So the control line should always appear if the strip is working correctly, while the test line only appears when hCG is actually present.

        Reaction site — holds free, mobile anti-hCG antibody linked to a dye enzyme
        Test site (T line) — fixed anti-hCG antibody; captures hCG–antibody complexes
        Control site (C line) — fixed anti-primary antibody; captures any free antibody
        TMB — dye substrate, converted from clear to blue by the enzyme
        hCG — human chorionic gonadotropin, the hormone being detected
        Capillary action — the passive flow that moves everything along the strip
